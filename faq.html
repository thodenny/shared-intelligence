FAQ

Common questions. Answers are my thoughts, not authoritative verdicts.

Is my data private when I use these tools?

+

It depends on the tool and your plan. By default, most free-tier AI tools use your conversations to improve their models. That means what you type can, in principle, be reviewed by humans at the company.

What to do: Check your account settings on whichever tool you're using — most have an option to opt out of training data sharing. On paid plans, data retention and opt-out controls are typically more explicit.

Rule of thumb: Don't paste genuinely sensitive information — customer data, confidential strategy, personal health details — into a free consumer AI tool. Use your judgment the same way you would with any cloud software you don't fully control.

Which tool should I pay for first?

+

Start free on two or three tools, find the one you open every day, then pay for that one. Don't subscribe to anything until you've built a habit with the free tier.

That said, if you're doing a lot of writing and analysis at work: Claude Pro ($20/month) is where most knowledge workers get the most consistent value — and is the one I use most often. Longer context window, priority access, and better performance on complex tasks.

If you need image generation or want one tool that does everything: Gemini or ChatGPT are solid options. I prefer Gemini's image creation.

Pick one. You don't need all three to get started.

Will AI replace my job?

+

I'm not sure, but your role will likely change. AI is already replacing specific tasks, not whole roles. First drafts, data summarization, research synthesis, scheduling, formatting — these are tasks AI handles well. That frees up time, but it also raises expectations.

The work that stays human: judgment calls with real stakes, relationships that require trust, accountability for outcomes, work that requires understanding context that isn't written down anywhere. Focus on developing those things. Use AI for everything else. This is how I'm approaching it.

Can I trust what AI tells me?

+

Trust it the same way you'd trust a very smart colleague who reads a lot but sometimes misremembers details. Useful, often right, occasionally confidently wrong.

AI models can hallucinate — generating plausible-sounding facts, citations, statistics, or quotes that don't exist. The output always sounds fluent and confident, which makes errors harder to spot than a Google result that just says "no results."

When to verify: Any specific fact, number, date, name, or citation that you'll act on or share publicly.

When to trust it: Writing, structure, brainstorming, summarizing content you already understand, explaining concepts, drafting communications. These are lower-risk uses — but still warrant a human review.

Do I need to know how to code to use AI effectively?

+

No. The entire premise of this site is that you don't. The most valuable AI skills for most professionals have nothing to do with code. Knowing what to ask, how to give useful context, and how to evaluate what comes back are the skills to learn.

That said, having a basic familiarity with how to read simple code is increasingly useful — to understand and verify what AI builds for you in tools like Replit or Zapier. But it's not a prerequisite to getting enormous value from AI right now.

What's the difference between free and paid plans?

+

Usually three things: model quality, usage limits, and features.

Free plans give you access to a capable but older or smaller model, with daily usage caps that cut you off when you hit them. Paid plans give you the latest, most capable model with higher message limits and access to extras like image generation, web search, or longer context windows.

For light or occasional use, free tiers are genuinely useful. If AI becomes a daily work tool, the $20/month for a paid plan pays for itself quickly. One hour of saved work per week at any professional salary justifies the cost.

Is there one "best" AI tool I should use?

+

No, and anyone who tells you otherwise is either selling something or hasn't used them all. Different tools genuinely excel at different things — Claude for writing and analysis, Perplexity for research, NotebookLM for your own documents, Gemini for Google Workspace users.

The "best" tool is the one you use. Start with one, build a habit, then add a second when you hit a gap the first one can't fill.

How much time does AI save?

+

It varies enormously by how you use it, but for professionals who build AI into their actual workflow, 2–5 hours per week is a reasonable expectation once you're past the learning curve. Some people save more. I personally save around 10 hours a week currently.

The savings tend to cluster in a few places: first drafts (writing that used to take an hour takes 10 minutes), research synthesis (summarizing documents you'd have skimmed), and prep work (agendas, talking points, status updates that pile up).

The catch is that early on, AI doesn't save time — it costs time. You're learning prompts, evaluating outputs, building judgment about what it's good at. Expect 2–4 weeks before you're genuinely faster. That investment is worth it.
